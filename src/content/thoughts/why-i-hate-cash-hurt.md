I fucking hate Johnny Cash's cover of *Hurt*. I don't mean I skip it when it comes on. I mean I find it artistically offensive, I find the near-universal adoration of it more offensive still, and I find the idea that Cash somehow improved on Trent Reznor's original close to incomprehensible.

This isn't tribalism. I love plenty of Johnny Cash's back catalogue, and I don't particularly like Nine Inch Nails; most of their music is shite. I've no attachment to Reznor and no interest in defending industrial rock against country. I just think *Hurt* is a work of genius, and Cash took the things that make it genius and fucking destroyed them.

## The tritone, you fucking philistines

Let's start with the music, since apparently somebody has to.

The vocal melody in Reznor's original leans hard on the tritone, that ugly, unstable interval that sounds wrong to most ears. It's uncomfortable and it fights you. You want something pleasant and familiar and the melody won't give it to you.

**That's the fucking hurt.** The dissonance isn't a flaw in an otherwise lovely tune that needs fixing. It's what the narrator is feeling, put into notes.

Then the chorus arrives, the dissonance resolves into consonance, and the whole thing opens up into something beautiful. The song doesn't just describe suffering; it makes you sit in the tension of it and then lets you feel the release. The chorus is only beautiful because of what came before it.

Cash smooths most of that out. The abrasive edges go, the melody gets easier on the ear, and **the hurt goes out of *Hurt*.** Somehow this gets called an improvement. It's like taking a painting built on the contrast between dark and light, painting over the dark bits, and congratulating yourself on making it prettier. You haven't improved the painting. You've shown you don't understand it.

## Confession is not regret

This is the part that really makes my blood boil.

Reznor's *Hurt* is a confession: unsolicited, ugly and deeply personal. The narrator knows what he's done and what he's become. He knows the pain he's caused and the pain he lives in. He isn't asking you to feel sorry for him, he isn't making excuses, and he isn't gazing back wistfully over a lifetime of unfortunate decisions waiting for someone to tell him it's all terribly sad. He's confronting himself.

And that's why I find the original comforting. Yes, comforting.

People treat *Hurt* as relentless nihilism, which I think is a staggeringly shallow reading of it. The hope is right there in the move from verse to chorus. You have to face your pain, own the reality you've made and stop hiding from what you've done, and only then is something better possible. I'm not saying all suffering is self-inflicted, or that you can just decide to feel better. But honesty and responsibility matter. The confession doesn't absolve him; it's where facing up to things starts. The music tells you this too: the dissonance is the reckoning and the consonance is the hope. It's beautiful because it won't pretend beauty comes without pain.

Now listen to Cash. An old man looking backwards, his weary voice soaked in regret, over a sentimental arrangement, with a video practically begging you to contemplate the tragic passage of time. Look at this poor old man. Look at everything he's lost. Isn't it sad?

**Fuck. Off.**

It's self-pity dressed up as profundity. The original asks you to face the consequences of your actions; the cover asks you to feel sorry for someone living with theirs. I despise that difference.

Regret is cheap. You can regret every shitty decision you've ever made without taking real responsibility for a single one. You can spend your whole life being a cunt, reach old age, stare mournfully into the middle distance and announce that you're terribly sorry. That doesn't make you profound. It makes you a cunt with regrets.

Reznor's narrator doesn't get to escape into that cosy retrospective self-pity. He has to live in what he's done, and the beauty is in his willingness to face it.

## The sentimentalisation of everything

What gets me most is the claim that Cash brought emotional depth to the song. As if Reznor had written a nasty, abrasive little industrial track and it took a wise old country singer to find the humanity buried underneath.

**The humanity was already there, you cloth-eared bastards.** It was in the composition: in the dissonance and the release, in the fragile, restrained delivery, in the gap between what the narrator says and what the music makes you feel.

Cash didn't discover any of that. He swapped a sophisticated musical expression of pain and hope for something far more conventional, an old man sounding sad. And because it's instantly accessible, because the emotional instructions are written in enormous letters across every frame of that mawkish video, everyone assumes it's deeper. It isn't. It's shallower. It's warm, fuzzy, emotionally manipulative cunt music that lets you enjoy being moved without asking you to face anything uncomfortable. The original makes you go through the pain before you get to the beauty. The cover hands you the sadness pre-packaged with a fucking ribbon on it.

## "It's not my song anymore"

Then, inevitably, somebody quotes Reznor: *"It's not my song anymore."* The killer argument. Even the man who wrote it admits Cash made the definitive version!

Except that's not what those words mean. They describe losing ownership of something intensely personal: it's been taken, transformed and so thoroughly attached to someone else that the person who made it no longer feels it's his. People hear an endorsement. I hear something much closer to dispossession.

To be fair, Reznor did end up speaking warmly about Cash's version, especially the video, and I'm not claiming he secretly hates it. My point is that his feelings about the cover don't decide whether it's any good, and the line everyone quotes doesn't say what they think it says. If someone took something I'd made, stripped out what I considered its most essential parts and got universal acclaim for improving it, I might not feel very possessive of the result either. That wouldn't mean I thought they'd improved it. It might mean I barely recognised it.

That quote gets wheeled out as proof of Cash's superiority so people don't have to engage with the actual fucking music.

## Music means something

I watch an unreasonable amount of music theory YouTube: Adam Neely, Tantacrul, and assorted other people who'll spend an hour explaining why a particular chord or interval makes you feel a particular way. I don't think music has to be dissected to be enjoyed, but I find the link between structure and meaning fascinating. A melody isn't just something to hang the words on, and harmony isn't just chords that sound nice together. The music tells a story of its own, and changing a few notes can change that story completely.

Look at what's been done to Leonard Cohen's *Hallelujah*: a song full of messy, often irreverent interplay between sex, faith, disappointment and its own musical structure, turned over and over into a generic anthem of sentimental reverence. People hear something beautiful, decide that making it prettier must make it better, strip out the complexity and the tension, and then congratulate themselves on finding its emotional heart. It's fucking maddening.

The thing is, I hated Cash's *Hurt* long before I knew enough theory to say why. I didn't sit down with a textbook, notice the missing tritone and decide I ought to be angry. I heard the cover, recoiled from what it had done to the original, and eventually learned enough to understand what I'd been hearing. The theory explains the hatred. It didn't create it.

## I know. I know.

I realise most normal people don't have this relationship with a cover version. They hear Johnny Cash singing a sad song, feel moved, and get on with their day. They don't spend twenty years nursing an ever more elaborate grievance about the destruction of harmonic storytelling, or hear a missing tritone and think about crimes against humanity. I mostly keep this to myself, because I suspect explaining it in person would make people quietly back away from me.

But this is my website, and it's my fucking hill to die on.

I like Johnny Cash. I don't like Nine Inch Nails. And I think Reznor's *Hurt* is one of the most extraordinary pieces of musical storytelling ever written, because it finds real beauty and hope in facing up to something ugly. Cash took that journey, removed the tension that makes it work, swapped accountability for sentimental regret, and got universal acclaim for it. That's not an improvement. It's a desecration, and I'm completely convinced I'm right.

**If you prefer Johnny Cash's version of *Hurt*, you're dead to me.**
